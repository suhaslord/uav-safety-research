# UNM Crazyflie experiment — concepts I should be able to explain

The simulator gives us one Crazyflie 2D trajectory. We then keep that trajectory fixed and ask what happens when position measurements become noisy, biased, or unavailable. Every case uses the same constant-velocity Kalman filter so we only change one thing at a time.

## State

The filter state is `[x, y, vx, vy]`: estimated horizontal position and horizontal velocity.

## Prediction step

Between measurements, the filter assumes velocity stays roughly constant for one small timestep. It predicts the next position from the current position and velocity. The process-noise covariance allows for the fact that the real vehicle can accelerate, so the constant-velocity model is not perfect.

## Measurement update

When a position measurement exists, the filter compares that measurement with its predicted position. That difference is the **innovation**. The Kalman gain combines prediction and measurement according to their uncertainty, correcting both position and velocity estimates.

## Noise

Gaussian position noise makes each measurement randomly too high or too low. As the injected noise sigma rises, the innovations become larger and the estimator RMSE rises. In the current sweep, mean estimator RMSE rises from about 0.022 m at 0.04 m noise sigma to about 0.170 m at 0.32 m sigma.

## Bias

Bias is different from random noise because the measurement stays shifted in one direction. A fixed +x offset can repeatedly pull the filter toward a wrong position because every biased measurement looks internally consistent. The fault-window RMSE rises from about 0.053 m for 0.05 m bias to about 0.406 m for 0.40 m bias.

## Dropout

During position dropout, there is no measurement update. The filter only predicts forward, so its covariance grows. If motion remains close to constant velocity, this can work surprisingly well for a short period. If the vehicle changes direction while the filter is prediction-only, the stored velocity becomes a bad model of the new motion and error can grow quickly.

## Strongest timing result

A fixed 2.0 s dropout is almost harmless during some steady segments but severe around maneuvers. For example, the 7–9 s dropout reaches only about 0.004 m maximum error, while the same 2.0 s dropout starting at the 10 s direction change reaches about 0.526 m maximum error.

The important conclusion is that **dropout severity depends on vehicle dynamics and estimator state, not only dropout duration**.

## Residual / innovation diagnostic

Residuals are useful when measurements still arrive. The normalized innovation squared (NIS) scales the innovation by the uncertainty the filter expects. Persistent large NIS means measurements and model predictions disagree more than the nominal run says they should.

In this experiment, the high-noise case and the +0.40 m bias case trigger the residual-based indicator, while nominal does not.

## Covariance diagnostic

During dropout there is no measurement, so there is no innovation to inspect. The filter's covariance is more useful: it grows as the filter predicts without correction. The 4 s dropout crosses the empirical uncertainty threshold shortly after dropout begins.

## What this does and does not claim

These are simulation-only results. The flight trajectory itself is generated by genuine Webots using the Bitcraze Crazyflie model and controller. The measurement faults are injected offline afterward so every estimator case sees the same underlying flight. This isolates the effect of one measurement degradation at a time; it is not a claim about physical-flight performance.
